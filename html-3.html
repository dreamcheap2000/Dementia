Pain in Dementia: Practical Clinical Synthesis

Causes, assessment, management (including image-guided interventions), and unresolved core gaps.

Pain is common and frequently underdetected in dementia because communication barriers increase as disease progresses. Untreated pain worsens agitation, sleep disruption, mobility decline, caregiver burden, and potentially inappropriate psychotropic use.

1) Why pain is missed in dementia

Neurocognitive barriers

  • Reduced verbal reporting and altered pain expression.
  • Executive/language deficits limit history quality.
  • Delirium overlap masks pain behaviors.

Clinical-system barriers

  • No universal workflow for observational pain tools.
  • Behavioral symptoms often treated before pain is assessed.
  • Fragmented handoff between primary, geriatrics, and pain services.

Typical consequences

  • Functional decline and immobility.
  • Falls, poorer sleep, malnutrition risk.
  • Potential overtreatment with antipsychotics/sedatives.

2) Common causes of pain in people with dementia

Musculoskeletal (most frequent)

  • Osteoarthritis (knee/hip/shoulder/spine)
  • Compression fractures, sarcopenia, contractures
  • Myofascial pain and immobility-related pain

Neuropathic & mixed

  • Diabetic neuropathy, postherpetic neuralgia
  • Radiculopathy/spinal stenosis
  • Post-stroke central pain

Visceral / medical

  • Constipation, urinary retention, pressure injury
  • Dental pain, occult infection, ischemic pain
  • Medication adverse effects (e.g., cramps)

Procedure/device related

  • Poorly fitted orthoses, catheter-related discomfort
  • Postoperative or post-fracture pain undertreatment
  • Pain during transfers/repositioning

3) Assessment framework (stepwise)

  1. Ask first (if possible): simple present-tense questions, body map, and movement-evoked pain.
  2. Observe behavior: facial expression, vocalization, guarding, resistance to care, sleep/appetite change.
  3. Use validated tools repeatedly (same tool, same shift context).
  4. Treat-then-reassess cycle: objective reassessment after intervention.
  5. Rule out urgent causes: fracture, retention, bowel obstruction, acute ischemia, infection, pressure injury.

Recognized sources: AGS pain guidance in older adults; NICE dementia guidance (NG97); American Psychiatric Association dementia-related agitation guidance; IASP older-person pain resources.

4) Interactive observational scales (copyable)

PAINAD (0–10)

Total: 0 / 10 (no-to-minimal observed pain)

Common practical interpretation: 1–3 mild, 4–6 moderate, 7–10 severe observed pain (institutional protocols vary).

Abbey Pain Scale (0–18)

Total: 0 / 18 (no pain)

Conventional Abbey categories: 0 no pain, 1–3 mild, 4–7 moderate, 8–13 severe, 14+ extreme.

5) Management: evidence-based, multimodal, goal-directed

Non-pharmacologic first-line (all stages)

  • Regular mobilization, PT/OT, seating/positioning optimization.
  • Heat/cold, massage, gentle manual therapy, sleep optimization.
  • Structured activity, music, and caregiver coaching for pain behaviors.
  • Address constipation, retention, footwear, pressure points, oral care.

Pharmacologic principles

  • Start low, go slow; schedule + rescue only if needed.
  • Acetaminophen often first choice (watch total daily dose/liver risk).
  • NSAIDs cautiously and briefly (renal/GI/CV risk in frail elders).
  • Opioids: reserve for moderate-severe pain with close bowel/falls/sedation monitoring.
  • Neuropathic pain: selected adjuvants (e.g., duloxetine/gabapentinoids) with cognition/fall surveillance.

7) Core unresolved issues / implementation gaps

Major gaps

  • Too few dementia-specific analgesic and interventional trials.
  • No single global standard for observational pain thresholds.
  • Inconsistent reassessment documentation after treatment changes.
  • Poor integration of pain metrics into behavioral-symptom pathways.

High-priority fixes

  • Mandatory pain check before escalating psychotropics.
  • Same-scale repeated scoring per shift + trend charts.
  • Caregiver training in pain behavior recognition.
  • Early referral pathways to geriatrics/pain/interventional services.
This educational module supports clinical reasoning and does not replace local protocols, prescribing standards, or specialist evaluation.
